\abstract{Equivariant neural networks are the standard architecture for predicting molecular and materials properties, and are called E(3)-equivariant. Many deployed models, however, are equivariant only to rotations, not reflections: their features carry no parity labels. Whether symmetry should be built in or learned is debated largely on rotations, in the currency of accuracy. On the reflection axis it differs: symmetry forces the piezoelectric tensor of a centrosymmetric crystal to vanish identically, so a nonzero prediction is not approximately wrong but physically impossible. Across matched pairs of three architectures whose sole intended difference is parity labelling, the parity-labelled arms stay below the threshold on all 2,000 centrosymmetric crystals; the rotation-only arms violate it for 90--96\%. Retraining on the exact answer and up-weighting it a hundredfold in the loss still leave two-thirds flagged: none of the training strategies we tested recovers the constraint. Of 18 released architectures six type parity, six are rotation-only, and six have no rank-3 tensor path at all; among the twelve that can produce a rank-3 output, only half type parity. Feature parity, not the training strategies tested here, decides whether symmetry-forbidden outputs occur.}
\keywords{equivariant neural networks, inductive bias, learned versus built-in symmetry, parity, O(3) versus SO(3) equivariance, piezoelectric tensor, centrosymmetric crystals, Neumann's principle}
